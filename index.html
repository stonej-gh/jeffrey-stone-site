Freedom to play

The October 1977 issue of Byte magazine arrived when I was a teenager building my own computer. Its cover theme was "Implementing Space War". I have written an almost embarrassing number of Spacewar games in the years since. Then, I got curious about machine learning. I am publishing the results in hopes that others will share the joy of discovery.

Spacewar: Orbital Duel

There on page 86 of that Byte was an article by Dave Kruglinski, "How to Implement Space War (or Using Your Oscilloscope as a Telescope)". Everything about the article fascinated me, starting with the fact that a '70s era microcomputer, 1/1000th of what we carry in our pockets today, could make the calculations for a physically realistic orbital mechanics simulation.

My own versions tracked Moore's law, each more sophisticated than the last. I developed a ladder of 20 programmed levels. The game became more fun and more challenging, and looked great with some aesthetic flourishes as a mobile app. Then came the neural networks. The spine-tingling excitement of building a system that does not need detailed programming, but can learn to play a video game by trial and error, is frankly addictive, and it has led to so much more. For a start, Spacewar: Orbital Duel is based on a hybrid AI opponent, a neural pilot with a few scripted guard rails. Your opponent learned by playing. I never told it how.

The labs

Machine learning

The Pilot →

The neural network that you are playing against in Spacewar: Orbital Duel uses reinforcement learning in a deep neural network. This technology broke out in 2006 to 2012, but is based on concepts that date back to the 1940s and 1950s.

The Spotter →

The Spotter lab can play Spacewar from nothing but what it sees on the screen, with a convolutional neural network that does image segmentation. It is based on the 2015 "U-Net" paper (Ronneberger et al.). CNNs took over image processing around 2012, built on ideas worked out in the '80s and '90s.

The Owl Coming soon

In the Owl lab, a transformer that was never told the name of a plant learns 24 of them from ten photos each, and the notebook shows what it looks at. The transformer (the T in GPT) and its attention mechanism bring the ML story very near to the present day. The Transformer paper, "Attention Is All You Need", was published by Google researchers in 2017. ChatGPT brought the approach to the mainstream in 2022. Rabbitbrush: Plant ID relies on the same kind of network to distinguish 4,000 plant species by their leaves, buds, flowers or bark.

Why free

I design high speed datapaths for a living: chips that encrypt, protect, compress, detect and calculate. Why would I then spend evenings and weekends designing labs and applications to give away for free? Quite simply, I am giving myself the freedom to follow wherever my curiosity leads.
